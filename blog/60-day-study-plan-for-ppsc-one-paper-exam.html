60-Day Study Plan for PPSC One Paper Exam

📅 October 31, 2026✍️ MCQs Tayyari Team⏱️ 9 min read

Sixty days is enough to prepare properly for a PPSC one paper exam — and short enough that every wasted week hurts. The paper itself is well known: 100 MCQs in 90 minutes, 100 marks, negative marking of −0.25 for each wrong answer, and a qualifying threshold of 40%. What most aspirants lack is not time or books. It is a plan that tells them what to study today, how many questions to practise, and when to stop reading and start revising.

This plan does exactly that. It assumes three to four hours a day, and divides the sixty days into three phases: build, strengthen, and sharpen. If you fall behind, catch up by cutting new reading, never by skipping revision.

First, Know What the Paper Rewards

A one paper test is roughly four-fifths post-related and general material and one-fifth general ability, but the exact mix depends on the post — check your advertisement first. The subjects that appear again and again are English, Urdu, Pakistan Studies, Islamiat, Everyday Science, Basic Mathematics, Current Affairs and General Knowledge, plus the post-related subject where the advertisement names one. A plan that ignores any one of these hands away marks before the exam begins.

Two facts should shape your approach: negative marking punishes blind guessing, so accuracy matters as much as speed; and a 100-question paper demands stamina, which only comes from practising under a clock from the first week.

The Three Phases at a Glance

Days 1–20 build your base: one subject block at a time, with MCQs after every topic. Days 21–40 strengthen it: mixed practice, your weakest subjects first, and your first full mocks. Days 41–60 sharpen it: almost no new material, only revision, mocks and your mistake notebook. The daily rhythm stays the same throughout — ninety minutes of reading, sixty minutes of MCQs, thirty minutes of revision — and only the content changes.

Keep one notebook from day one: every wrong answer goes in it, with the correct answer and one line explaining why. By day 60 it holds exactly the facts you personally keep forgetting.

Phase 1 — Days 1–20: Build the Base

Cover two subjects a week: English and Urdu first, Pakistan Studies and Islamiat second, Everyday Science and Basic Mathematics next, then Current Affairs and General Knowledge. Read a topic, then immediately attempt 30 to 50 MCQs on it — reading without questions feels productive and teaches almost nothing. Use English MCQs, Urdu MCQs, Pakistan Studies MCQs, Everyday Science MCQs and Basic Mathematics MCQs as you go.

For Current Affairs, cover the last six months properly — national developments, key appointments, international events involving Pakistan, sports and awards — and keep a one-page list you update weekly. For mathematics, master the recurring short cuts: percentages, ratios, averages, speed and time, and basic algebra, which regularly carry ten marks or more.

End each day by noting the five facts you most want to remember tomorrow, and each week with a 50-question mixed test of that week's subjects. A subject below half marks gets the first hour of the following week — not the last.

Phase 2 — Days 21–40: Strengthen and Test Yourself

Now new reading drops to one hour a day and mixed practice takes over. Each morning, attempt 100 MCQs from all subjects, and spend the evening only on what went wrong. Revise your mistake notebook every third day, and watch the same errors disappear.

Take one full mock a week under real conditions: 90 minutes, no phone, no pausing, negative marking applied. Watch attempts as well as scores — attempting 85 with 60 correct beats attempting all 100 with 55 correct, because every wild guess costs a quarter mark. Try a full-length mock at the exam simulator and work through the PPSC exam section for post-wise practice.

By day 40, name honestly the two subjects costing you the most marks: they own the first ten days of phase 3.

Phase 3 — Days 41–60: Sharpen, Don't Expand

The biggest mistake in the final twenty days is opening a new book. Your job now is revision: the mistake notebook, Current Affairs list, mathematics formulas, Urdu mutazad and muradif pairs, and high-frequency Pakistan Studies and Islamiat facts. Read less, recall more — cover the answer, say it aloud, then check.

Take a full mock every third day — six in this phase — and spend the day after each mock on nothing but its mistakes. Between mocks, drill daily: 30 English questions, 20 mathematics questions, one pass over Current Affairs. In the last three days, stop mocks entirely: light revision, early nights, documents ready the evening before.

A Sample Day (Working Aspirants Included)

If you work, the plan still fits: an hour before work for MCQs, an hour after for reading, thirty minutes before bed for revision. A phase 2 weekday: morning, 50 mixed MCQs; evening, one weak topic plus its questions; night, mistake notebook and Current Affairs list. On a free day, double the MCQ count. Six days at three hours beat one day at ten followed by three days off.

Keep Sunday evenings free: a plan you can sustain for sixty days beats a perfect plan abandoned in week two.

5 Mistakes That Break a 60-Day Plan

  1. Reading without practising. Every topic must end in questions, or it will not survive until exam day.
  2. Starting mocks too late. If your first full paper is in the final week, speed and stamina problems have nowhere left to be fixed.
  3. Adding new books in the final phase. New material in days 41–60 displaces revision of what you already know.
  4. Guessing without a rule. Attempt a question only when you can eliminate at least two options; otherwise, leave it and save the quarter mark.
  5. Skipping the weak subject. The subject you avoid is usually the one carrying the most recoverable marks.

Sixty days from now, the exam will arrive whether you prepared or not. Start today, keep the daily MCQ target sacred, and let the plan — not your mood — decide what you study. Read our PPSC one paper preparation guide, then open free practice MCQs and begin day one.

Key takeaways
  • 60 days split into three phases: build (days 1–20), strengthen (days 21–40), sharpen (days 41–60).
  • Daily rhythm: 90 minutes reading, 60 minutes MCQs, 30 minutes revision — three to four hours a day.
  • Every topic ends in 30–50 MCQs; every wrong answer goes into a mistake notebook.
  • Full 90-minute mocks with −0.25 negative marking start in week four and run every third day in the final phase.
  • Final 20 days mean revision only — no new books, no new topics.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 60 days enough for PPSC one paper preparation?

Yes, with three to four focused hours a day: enough to cover the full subject range, practise mixed MCQs and mocks, and still leave a dedicated revision phase.

How many MCQs should I practise each day?

Aim for 50 to 100 a day: 30 to 50 per new topic in phase one, rising to 100 mixed MCQs a day in phase two. Review every wrong answer the same day.

When should I start taking full mock tests?

Take your first full 100-question, 90-minute mock in week four, then one a week until day 40, and one every third day in the final twenty days, always applying −0.25 negative marking.

What should I study in the last week before the exam?

Only revision: mistake notebook, Current Affairs notes, mathematics formulas and high-frequency facts. Stop mocks three days before the test and keep the final days light.
