SPSC Preparation Guide 2026: Syllabus & Strategy

📅 October 29, 2026✍️ MCQs Tayyari Team⏱️ 8 min read

If you are aiming for a Sindh government job, the Sindh Public Service Commission (SPSC) is the gatekeeper — and its tests reward a very specific kind of preparation. Many aspirants study hard for months and still miss the cut, not from lack of knowledge, but because they prepared for the wrong paper: a general syllabus when their post demanded a professional one, or a screening test treated like a written exam. This guide explains how SPSC tests work, what the syllabus covers, and how to build a plan that fits real life.

One honest warning first: SPSC does not run one single paper for every post. The pattern, syllabus, and weightage change with the advertisement, so read your own advertisement's syllabus before opening any book. What follows shows the common patterns and how to adapt them.

First, Know Which SPSC Track You Are On

Most aspirants fall into one of three tracks, each needing different preparation. Getting this wrong is the costliest mistake, so sort it out on day one.

  • General recruitment posts: for posts like assistants. Selection normally starts with one MCQ screening or written test on general subjects, then an interview. Most of this guide is for this track.
  • Specialist and lecturer posts: your own subject dominates. Published syllabi commonly give around 60% weightage to professional knowledge, the rest to general knowledge and English.
  • The Combined Competitive Examination (CCE): Sindh's provincial civil service track, run in stages — MCQ screening, then descriptive written papers in the CSS style, then a viva voce. CCE needs essay and precis practice alongside MCQs.

Place yourself in one track before planning anything. A general-test plan will not carry a lecturer candidate, and a CCE candidate who only practises MCQs will be unprepared for the written stage even after clearing screening.

The Screening Test Syllabus, Section by Section

For general recruitment, the commonly published screening syllabus divides the paper into five equal areas. Tests are typically 100 MCQs in about 90 minutes, so speed matters as much as knowledge. The five areas are:

  • English (20%): grammar, vocabulary, comprehension — synonyms, antonyms, prepositions, idioms, sentence correction, voice, narration.
  • Current Affairs (20%): recent national and Sindh-specific developments from roughly the last year.
  • Pakistan Affairs (20%): the Pakistan movement, constitutional history, geography, and the country's political story, with a Sindh angle covered below.
  • General Ability (20%): quantitative reasoning and mental ability — percentages, ratios, averages, series, simple logic.
  • Everyday Science (20%): basic scientific literacy — the human body, vitamins, inventions, units, the natural world.

Because the split is even, no section can be ignored. Aspirants usually over-invest in the subject they enjoy and quietly abandon one-fifth of the paper. Do the opposite: give your weakest section the first hour of every study day, and let your strongest section run on revision alone.

Subject-Wise Strategy for SPSC

English — the section that separates qualifiers

English is where screening tests are often won or lost, because most aspirants leave it to chance. Treat it as a pattern test: learn the rules behind prepositions and tenses, and build vocabulary in daily sets of ten words. Practise in English MCQs, and note every rule you get wrong — that list is your final-week revision sheet.

Pakistan Affairs — add the Sindh layer

The standard syllabus is only half the story for an SPSC paper. Examiners also draw on Sindh's history, geography, and institutions: the Indus river system, districts and divisions, cultural heritage, and the provincial setup. Study the national syllabus first, then add a Sindh layer — a short fact list, revised weekly. Our Pakistan Studies MCQs cover the national core; build your Sindh notes alongside them.

Current Affairs — the last twelve months, done daily

Current Affairs cannot be crammed in a week. Spend fifteen minutes a day on the day's developments, note them in one running document, and review the week's notes every Sunday. Give Sindh news the same attention as national news — budgets, schemes, and appointments appear in SPSC papers more often than aspirants expect. Practise with Current Affairs MCQs to turn reading into recall.

Everyday Science and General Ability — easy marks, if practised

Together these sections are 40% of a typical screening paper, and the most predictable part of it. Science repeats the same clusters — vitamins and diseases, SI units, inventions, the solar system — while General Ability repeats the same arithmetic patterns. Both reward short daily MCQ practice over textbook reading. Use Everyday Science MCQs and Basic Mathematics MCQs in timed sets.

A 30-Day Study Plan That Fits Around a Job

Most SPSC aspirants work or study full-time, so this plan assumes two focused hours a day: build the base in weeks one and two, then switch to timed practice in weeks three and four.

  1. Days 1–7: foundations. One section per day on rotation, ending each day with 30 MCQs from that section.
  2. Days 8–14: Sindh layer and weak sections. Add your Sindh notes, double time on your lowest-scoring section, and start a mistake notebook.
  3. Days 15–21: timed practice. One 100-MCQ timed mock every alternate day — no pausing, no phone. Off days go only to the mistakes those mocks exposed.
  4. Days 22–30: revision. No new books. Re-read your mistake notebook twice, revise the Sindh fact list, take two final mocks, and in the last three days revise only notes.

If your test date is closer than 30 days, compress, do not skip: halve the foundations week, but never cut the timed mocks. Exam speed only comes from practising under a clock.

5 Mistakes That Cost SPSC Aspirants Their Selection

  1. Preparing for the wrong track. A lecturer candidate revising only general subjects, or a CCE candidate ignoring essay writing, has lost before the test begins.
  2. Ignoring one section entirely. With five equal sections, a zero in any one needs near-perfection elsewhere. Weak sections get the first hour.
  3. Reading without practising. Every hour of reading needs a matching set of MCQs, or knowledge never becomes exam speed.
  4. Skipping Sindh-specific preparation. National material alone leaves the provincial questions unanswered.
  5. Never attempting a full mock. Chapter-wise practice alone means you discover your time problem in the exam hall. Take full-length timed mocks.

A final word on honesty: beware of any book or website claiming to sell "official" SPSC past papers. The reliable route is the published syllabus for your post, subject-wise MCQ practice, and timed mocks. See the SPSC exam section for practice material.

Key takeaways
  • Identify your track first: general recruitment, specialist/lecturer, or CCE — each needs a different plan.
  • The screening syllabus splits evenly across English, Current Affairs, Pakistan Affairs, General Ability, and Everyday Science.
  • Add a Sindh layer: the Indus system, districts, heritage, and provincial administration.
  • Two hours a day is enough: foundations, then timed 100-MCQ mocks, then mistake-notebook revision only.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the SPSC screening test pattern?

Commonly 100 MCQs in about 90 minutes — but the exact pattern and any negative marking depend on your post's advertisement, since SPSC issues post-wise syllabi. Always confirm against it.

Is the SPSC syllabus the same for every post?

No. General posts follow the five-section screening syllabus, while specialist and lecturer posts give roughly 60% weightage to the professional subject. CCE follows a separate multi-stage pattern with written papers and a viva voce.

How important is Sindh-specific knowledge for SPSC?

Important. SPSC papers regularly test Sindh's geography, history, culture, and provincial administration — a short, regularly revised Sindh fact list is a high-return habit.
