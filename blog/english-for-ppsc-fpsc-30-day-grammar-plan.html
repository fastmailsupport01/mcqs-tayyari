How to Prepare English for PPSC & FPSC: A 30-Day Grammar Plan

📅 October 13, 2026✍️ MCQs Tayyari Team⏱️ 8 min read

Ask any PPSC or FPSC topper which section won them their seat, and a surprising number will give the same answer: English. Not the post-specific syllabus, not current affairs — English. The English portion of the General Ability section carries 15–25 marks of almost identical grammar, paper after paper. It is also the section where average students quietly bleed marks they should never lose — not because English is hard, but because nobody ever taught them how to prepare for it the right way.

Here is the truth most aspirants miss: English in these exams is not a language test. It is a pattern test. The same rules — tenses, prepositions, articles, voice, narration, error detection — appear in every single paper, sometimes with barely changed sentences. You don't need to speak like a native. You need 30 days, a rulebook, and a method. This plan gives you all three.

What English Actually Looks Like in PPSC & FPSC Papers

Both the PPSC one paper test and the FPSC screening test are 100-MCQ papers, and their English portions are built from the same recurring question types:

  • Error detection — spot the grammatical mistake in a sentence (the examiners' favourite)
  • Fill in the blank — choose the correct preposition, article, or verb form
  • Active–passive voice and direct–indirect narration conversions
  • Synonyms, antonyms, one-word substitution, and idioms
  • Spellings and sentence-completion questions

Notice the split: roughly 70% grammar, 30% vocabulary. Grammar is rule-based and learnable in weeks — that is where this plan spends most of its energy. Vocabulary compounds slowly through exposure, so the plan builds it as a daily habit rather than a cramming subject.

The 30-Day Plan at a Glance

  • Daily time: 60–90 minutes — no more, no less. Consistency beats intensity.
  • Rule: every grammar topic = learn the rule in 20 minutes, then solve 30–50 MCQs on it the same day.
  • Tool: an error notebook — a plain register where every mistake you make gets written down with the correct rule. This notebook becomes your entire revision material in week 4.
  • Practice source: our free English MCQs section and past-paper-style questions.

Week 1 (Days 1–7): Tenses & Sentence Structure

Tenses are the foundation everything else stands on. If your tenses are shaky, voice, narration and error detection will all feel impossible — so we fix this first.

  • Days 1–2: Present tenses. Present simple, continuous, and perfect — focus on usage rules, not just forms. Learn the signal words: since/for, already, just, ever, never. Half of all tense questions are decided by these five words.
  • Days 3–4: Past tenses. Past simple vs. past continuous vs. past perfect. Master the one rule that matters: past perfect is for the earlier of two past events.
  • Day 5: Future forms & conditionals. Will vs. going to, and the three conditional types — examiners love Type 3 ("If I had studied, I would have passed").
  • Days 6–7: Volume. Solve 150 mixed tense MCQs. Every wrong answer goes into the error notebook with the rule, not just the answer.

Mentor tip: if you can't explain why an answer is correct, you haven't learned it. Copy the rule into your notebook, not just the option letter.

Week 2 (Days 8–14): Prepositions, Articles & Error Detection

These two small topics are the highest-yield real estate in the whole English section. A preposition or article question takes ten seconds to answer — if you've seen the rule before.

  • Days 8–10: Prepositions. Memorise the fixed-preposition list that repeats endlessly: depend on, consist of, accused of, good at, married to, died of/from. Then learn the time/place logic — in for months and years, on for days, at for exact times.
  • Days 11–12: Articles. The for superlatives, unique things (the sun, the Quran), and musical instruments; a/an for first mentions and professions. Learn the ten standard situations and you will rarely miss one.
  • Days 13–14: Error detection technique. This is the exam's favourite format, so learn the scanning order: (1) subject–verb agreement, (2) tense consistency, (3) preposition, (4) article. Run every sentence through this checklist and most errors jump out on their own. Solve 200 mixed MCQs across the week.

Week 3 (Days 15–21): Voice, Narration & the Vocabulary Engine

  • Days 15–17: Active–passive voice. Make one tense-wise conversion table and memorise it cold — passive is mechanical, and once the table is in your head, these are free marks. Don't forget imperatives and modals ("You should do it" → "It should be done").
  • Days 18–19: Narration. Three things to master: tense backshift (present → past), time-word changes (today → that day, tomorrow → the next day), and pronoun shifts. Practise with 100 MCQs.
  • Days 20–21: The vocabulary engine. Forget memorising random word lists. Learn 15 words a day from past papers — words that have actually appeared. For each word: one synonym, one antonym, one sentence you write yourself. Add 5 idioms daily the same way. Past-paper vocabulary repeats far more than aspirants believe.

By the end of week 3 you will have covered every grammar topic that appears in these papers. Nothing in week 4 is new — it is all about speed and accuracy.

Week 4 (Days 22–30): Timed Sets, Mocks & the Error Notebook

  • Days 22–24: Timed topic sets. 40 MCQs in 30 minutes per topic. You are training your pace now — no more untimed practice.
  • Days 25–27: Full English sections. Attempt complete English portions under exam conditions. After each, revise only your error notebook — by now it holds every personal weakness you have.
  • Days 28–30: Full mock tests. Sit full 100-MCQ papers on our exam simulator with the real timer and negative marking. The last two days: no new material, just the notebook and your formula sheet.

Learn the elimination habit during these mocks: cross out two obviously wrong options first, then decide. If you can't eliminate at least two, skip the question — with −0.25 negative marking, a blind guess costs you more than a skip.

5 Mistakes That Kill English Scores

  1. Reading grammar books cover to cover instead of solving MCQs. This is an MCQ exam — preparation must be MCQ-shaped.
  2. Memorising vocabulary lists without sentences. A word you can't use in a sentence is a word you'll forget in the hall.
  3. Skipping error detection practice. It's the single most repeated format — and the most trainable.
  4. No error notebook. Without one, you will make the same mistakes in week 4 that you made in week 1.
  5. Translating from Urdu while solving. Grammar rules decide answers, not translations. Trust the rule you learned.
Key takeaways
  • English in PPSC/FPSC is a pattern test: ~70% grammar, ~30% vocabulary — and the grammar repeats every paper.
  • Tenses → prepositions/articles → voice/narration → timed mocks: one topic block per week.
  • Maintain an error notebook from day 1 — it becomes your entire revision material in week 4.
  • Train at exam pace with negative marking; eliminate two options before attempting, skip what you can't.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I really prepare English in 30 days from scratch?

Yes — if your basics aren't zero. This plan assumes you can read and write basic English. With 60–90 focused minutes daily, the rule-based grammar portion is fully coverable in 30 days. If you're starting from absolute zero, give yourself 45 days using the same weekly structure.

Which English book is best for PPSC/FPSC preparation?

For rules, any standard grammar book works — Wren & Martin or High School English Grammar for the core rules. But books alone never suffice: pair them with daily MCQ practice and past-paper questions, because the exam tests rule application, not rule knowledge.

How many English questions come in the PPSC one paper exam?

The English portion of the General Ability part typically carries 15–25 marks, varying slightly by post and advertisement. That is nearly a quarter of the paper from one learnable subject — which is exactly why toppers treat it as their scoring engine.
