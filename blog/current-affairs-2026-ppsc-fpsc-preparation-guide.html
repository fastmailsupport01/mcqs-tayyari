Current Affairs 2026: How to Prepare for PPSC & FPSC

📅 October 18, 2026✍️ MCQs Tayyari Team⏱️ 8 min read

Of all the subjects in a PPSC or FPSC paper, current affairs is the strangest one. You can't "finish" it like Islamiat or English grammar. New events happen every single day, and every aspirant secretly worries the same thing: how much is enough? Should you read the newspaper for an hour daily? Memorise every cabinet reshuffle? Track every international summit?

Relax. Current affairs is genuinely one of the highest return-per-hour sections in these exams — if you prepare it the right way. Examiners don't test whether you read everything; they test whether you noticed the important things. This guide shows you exactly what counts, where to read it, and the simple system that turns daily news into exam-day marks.

What "Current Affairs" Actually Means in PPSC & FPSC

First, kill the biggest misconception: you don't need to know everything that happened in 2026. In PPSC one paper and FPSC screening tests, current affairs questions almost always come from a 10–12 month window before the exam date, and they cluster around a surprisingly predictable set of themes:

  • Pakistan: federal cabinet and provincial governments, judiciary (Chief Justice, important verdicts), military leadership, major legislation, economic milestones, CPEC developments
  • World: heads of state/government of major countries, international summits (UN, OIC, SCO, G20), Pakistan's foreign visits and treaties
  • Sports: recent World Cups, Olympics/Asian Games results, Pakistan's cricket milestones, famous firsts
  • Science & defence: Pakistan's missile/space programme milestones, major global discoveries, Nobel Prizes of the current year
  • Awards & firsts: who won what, who became the first/largest/oldest — examiners love superlatives
  • Economy & finance: budget figures, SBP policy rate changes, IMF programme status, remittance records

Notice the pattern: examiners ask about events with a name, a number, or a first — appointments, records, awards, summits, figures. Gossip, opinions, and analysis never become MCQs. The moment you start filtering news through this lens, the subject shrinks to a manageable size.

The 15-Minute Daily System (Your Only Real Source)

Forget marathon newspaper reading. The toppers' system is boringly simple and takes 15 minutes a day:

  1. Skim the headlines of one reliable Pakistani news source (Dawn or Express Tribune) — just the front page and national section. You're hunting for names, numbers, and firsts, not reading editorials.
  2. Capture only 3–5 items per day in a dedicated current affairs notebook (physical or digital). Write each as a single factual line: "Pakistan's foreign exchange reserves crossed $X in [month]" — never paragraphs.
  3. Convert to MCQs every Sunday: turn each captured fact into a question with 4 options. Writing the wrong options yourself is what fixes the fact in memory — this single habit beats re-reading notes five times.

That's it. No monthly magazines are required if you do this daily, though a monthly current affairs digest (Jahangir's World Times or a similar compilation) makes an excellent safety net — read one digest a month and cross-check it against your notebook.

Month-by-Month: How to Cover 2026 Smartly

If your exam is approaching and you haven't been keeping a notebook, don't panic-read the whole year. Work backwards from your exam date:

  • Priority 1 (must know): the most recent 3–4 months in detail — appointments, summits, sports events, awards, budget figures.
  • Priority 2 (should know): months 4–8 before the exam — major events only, captured from a monthly digest.
  • Priority 3 (nice to know): months 8–12 — only the genuinely big stories (elections, wars, Nobel Prizes, record-breaking events).

Why backwards? Because recent events are disproportionately likely to appear — examiners write papers close to the exam date, and fresh events are fresh in their minds too.

Practice MCQs, Not Just News

Reading news builds awareness; solving MCQs builds exam skill. The two are different. A fact you merely read is a fact you'll forget under pressure. So after building your notebook, drill it: attempt our free Current Affairs MCQs regularly, and add any question you get wrong to your error notebook with its explanation.

Also note how current affairs overlaps with other subjects — a question about the current Chief Justice is also Pak Studies; the Nobel Prize winners belong to Everyday Science territory too. Current affairs preparation quietly strengthens three subjects at once, which is exactly why it pays so well per hour.

5 Mistakes That Waste Your Current Affairs Effort

  1. Reading without capturing. News you don't write down evaporates. If it took no notebook space, it will take no exam marks.
  2. Chasing every minor story. District-level transfers, routine statements, and political rhetoric are not MCQ material. Filter ruthlessly.
  3. Starting "from January" a week before the exam. Work backwards from the exam date; the last 3 months matter most.
  4. Memorising without MCQ practice. Facts you haven't answered as questions feel familiar but won't surface in the hall.
  5. Ignoring revision. Current affairs has the fastest forgetting curve of any subject — unrevised notes are as good as unread.

The Weekly Revision Loop

Because this subject forgets fast, build a loop into your routine: Sunday — convert the week's notes into MCQs and solve them; first Sunday of each month — re-solve all four weeks' MCQs plus the monthly digest; the final week before the exam — only your error notebook and the last 3 months. This loop takes under an hour a week and is the difference between "I read it once" and "I know it cold."

Key takeaways
  • Cover a 10–12 month window, working backwards — the most recent 3–4 months matter most.
  • Filter news for names, numbers, and firsts; ignore opinions and routine statements.
  • 15 minutes daily: capture 3–5 facts, then convert each into an MCQ every Sunday.
  • Revise weekly — current affairs has the fastest forgetting curve of all subjects.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many months of current affairs should I prepare for PPSC?

Prepare roughly the last 10–12 months before your exam, with the most recent 3–4 months in the greatest detail. Working backwards from the exam date beats studying chronologically from January.

Is the newspaper necessary, or are monthly digests enough?

A monthly digest alone is enough for most candidates if read carefully and converted into MCQs. The daily newspaper adds an edge for the very recent months — 15 minutes of headline skimming is plenty; skip the editorials.

Do current affairs questions differ between PPSC and FPSC?

The themes are nearly identical — national appointments, summits, sports, awards, economy. FPSC screening tests sometimes lean slightly more toward international affairs, while PPSC one paper papers often include more Pakistan-specific events. Prepare both together; the overlap is around 90%.
