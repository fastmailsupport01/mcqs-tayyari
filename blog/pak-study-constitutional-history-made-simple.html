Pak Study: Constitutional History Made Simple

📅 November 3, 2026✍️ MCQs Tayyari Team⏱️ 9 min read

Ask a PPSC aspirant which Pak Studies topic they dread most, and constitutional history usually tops the list. Dates blur together, three constitutions sound alike, and amendments feel endless. So many students skip the topic or cram it the night before — and both choices cost marks.

Here is the good news: constitutional history is one of the most predictable topics in the paper. Examiners do not ask obscure legal detail — just the same handful of facts: which constitution came when, what system it set up, why it ended, and which amendment changed what. Learn the story once, in order, and the MCQs almost answer themselves.

Why Examiners Love Constitutional History

In the PPSC one paper exam — 100 MCQs in 90 minutes, 100 marks, −0.25 per wrong answer, 40% to qualify — Pak Studies carries a steady share, and constitutional questions are among its easiest marks. They are fact-based: a date is either right or wrong, and a prepared student answers in seconds. That is why toppers never skip this topic.

The topic also connects to the rest of Pak Studies: the Objectives Resolution explains later constitutions' Islamic character, the 1973 Constitution explains today's federal structure, and the 18th Amendment explains provincial control of subjects once held centrally.

The Starting Point: 1947 to 1956, Nine Years Without a Constitution

Pakistan began life in 1947 without a constitution of its own. Until one could be written, the country was governed under the Government of India Act 1935, adapted as an interim constitution, with the Governor-General holding wide powers. A permanent constitution took nine years — delayed by disagreement over Islam's role, the East–West balance, and language.

The first landmark came on 12 March 1949, when the Constituent Assembly passed the Objectives Resolution, moved by Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan. It declared that sovereignty belongs to Allah, that authority would be exercised through the people's chosen representatives, and that fundamental rights, an independent judiciary and minority protection would be guaranteed. Examiners love asking who moved it and when it passed.

Progress was slow. The first Constituent Assembly was dissolved in 1954, challenged in the famous Molvi Tamizuddin case. The Bogra Formula of 1953 tried to settle representation between the two wings, and the One Unit scheme of 1955 merged West Pakistan's provinces into one unit to balance East Pakistan's larger population.

The 1956 Constitution: Pakistan's First, and Shortest-Lived

Pakistan's first constitution was adopted on 29 February 1956 and came into force on 23 March 1956 — a date still celebrated as Pakistan Day. It declared Pakistan an Islamic Republic, set up a parliamentary system (President as head of state, Prime Minister heading the government) with a single house, the National Assembly, recognised Urdu and Bengali as national languages, and adopted the Objectives Resolution as its guiding preamble.

It lasted barely two years. Political instability and centre–province friction weakened it, and in October 1958 it was abrogated when martial law was imposed. The exam trap is simple: students mix up 1956 with 1973. Fix it with one line — 1956 was the first, parliamentary, and it died in 1958.

The 1962 Constitution: The Presidential Experiment

The second constitution, promulgated in March 1962 and enforced from 8 June 1962, went the opposite way: a presidential system. The President held real executive power, and the legislature was elected indirectly through Basic Democracies — roughly 80,000 Basic Democrats as the electoral college. Associated with the Ayub Khan era, it too was abrogated in 1969 when martial law returned.

For MCQs, the 1962 constitution has one unmistakable fingerprint — presidential system plus Basic Democracies. If both appear in a question, the answer is 1962, not 1956 or 1973.

The 1973 Constitution: The One That Survived

The third constitution matters most, because it is still in force today. Passed on 10 April 1973 and enforced from 14 August 1973, it restored the parliamentary system, made Pakistan a federal republic, and declared Islam the state religion. The Prime Minister became chief executive, the President ceremonial head of state, and the Objectives Resolution became a substantive part of the constitution.

Its structure, in broad strokes: a federal Parliament (National Assembly and Senate), provincial assemblies, an independent judiciary, and fundamental rights. Examiners rarely ask article numbers, but they do ask the system (parliamentary, federal), the date (1973), and the character (Islamic, democratic, federal).

Why did 1973 survive where its predecessors failed? It was passed unanimously, giving it broad legitimacy — a favourite MCQ fact — and it balanced centre and provinces more carefully.

The Amendments That Examiners Actually Ask About

Amendments sound endless, but only a few appear regularly. The 8th Amendment (1985) gave the President power to dissolve the National Assembly — Article 58(2)(b) — used repeatedly through the 1990s. The 13th (1997) took it away; the 17th (2003) brought a version back. The 18th (2010) removed it again and went further: provinces gained real autonomy, the concurrent list was abolished, and NWFP became Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.

Learn them as a tug-of-war: 8th gives power, 13th takes it away, 17th gives some back, 18th takes it away for good and empowers the provinces. Provinces autonomy? 18th. Article 58(2)(b)? 8th.

5 Traps That Cost Marks on This Topic

  1. Mixing up 1956 and 1973. Both are parliamentary, but 1956 was the first and died in 1958.
  2. Calling 1962 parliamentary. It was presidential — Basic Democracies is the giveaway.
  3. Forgetting the interim setup. Before 1956, Pakistan ran under the adapted Government of India Act 1935.
  4. Confusing amendment directions. 8th gave dissolution power; 13th removed it; 18th empowered the provinces.
  5. Memorising dates without the story. Learn why each constitution ended, not just when.

Key takeaways
  • 1947–1956: interim rule under the Government of India Act 1935; Objectives Resolution, 12 March 1949.
  • 1956: first constitution, parliamentary, Islamic Republic — abrogated 1958.
  • 1962: presidential system, Basic Democracies — abrogated 1969.
  • 1973: parliamentary, federal, still in force — passed 10 April, enforced 14 August 1973.
  • Amendments: 8th gave dissolution power, 13th removed it, 18th (2010) empowered provinces.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which is Pakistan's first constitution?

The Constitution of 1956, adopted 29 February and enforced 23 March 1956. It made Pakistan an Islamic Republic under a parliamentary system.

Which constitution is currently in force in Pakistan?

The Constitution of 1973 — parliamentary and federal, as amended, most importantly by the 18th Amendment of 2010.

What was the Objectives Resolution?

A resolution passed on 12 March 1949, moved by Liaquat Ali Khan, setting out the future constitution's guiding principles — sovereignty belongs to Allah, with fundamental rights and an independent judiciary protected.

What did the 18th Amendment change?

It removed the President's dissolution power, abolished the concurrent list, gave provinces greater autonomy, and renamed NWFP as Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.
