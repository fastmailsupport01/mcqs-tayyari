English Vocabulary for Competitive Exams: Smart Techniques

📅 November 2, 2026✍️ MCQs Tayyari Team⏱️ 9 min read

Most PPSC and FPSC aspirants treat English vocabulary like a mountain: huge and impossible to climb before the exam. So they memorise long word lists for a week and forget them, or skip vocabulary and hope grammar carries them. Neither works. Vocabulary is a daily habit, and twenty minutes a day builds a word bank that survives until exam day.

In the PPSC one paper exam — 100 MCQs in 90 minutes, 100 marks, −0.25 per wrong answer, 40% to qualify — English carries a solid share through synonyms, antonyms, one-word substitutions and idioms. Quick marks if you know the word, lost marks if you do not. This guide gives you techniques to learn words so they stay.

Why Vocabulary Decides Marks in Competitive Exams

Vocabulary questions are the fastest on the paper. Grammar needs analysis; vocabulary needs recognition. If the word is in your memory, you answer in five seconds and bank the mark. If it is not, you guess — and with negative marking, guessing costs you. That is why toppers treat vocabulary as free marks.

Words also repeat across papers. Exams draw from a stable pool of formal English — "candid", "obscure", "diligent", "mitigate" appear again and again. Learn the repeated pool once, and it pays off in every exam you attempt. Your job is not to learn the whole dictionary, only the exam's vocabulary, deeply.

Learn Word Families, Not Word Lists

The biggest mistake aspirants make is memorising isolated words: a list of fifty words, each with a one-line meaning, read once and forgotten. Your brain stores words in networks, not in lists. So learn words in families — groups connected by meaning, root or theme — and each word strengthens the others.

Take the family around honesty: candid, frank, straightforward, transparent, blunt. Learn them together, note the small differences (candid is open, blunt is rude), and you own five words for the effort of one idea. Do the same for praise and criticism (commend, applaud, rebuke, condemn) and strength and weakness (robust, sturdy, fragile, feeble). When a synonym question appears, the whole family comes to mind.

Roots, Prefixes and Suffixes: The Shortcut That Multiplies Words

If word families are the network, roots are the master key. Much exam vocabulary comes from Latin and Greek roots, and one root unlocks many words at once. "Dict" means say: predict, dictate, verdict, contradict, dictionary. "Scrib" means write: describe, prescribe, inscribe, manuscript. Learn one root, and five words become obvious.

Prefixes and suffixes work the same way. "Un-", "in-", "dis-" and "mis-" flip meanings; "-able", "-tion" and "-ness" change a word's form. Spend one week on the thirty most common roots and prefixes, and you will start guessing unknown words correctly. No list can cover every word an examiner might choose — but roots cover the patterns behind them.

The 20-Word Daily System That Actually Sticks

Forget hundred-word days. The system that works is twenty words a day, done properly. For each word, write four things: the word, a short meaning in your own words, one example sentence you made yourself, and one synonym and one antonym. Your own sentence is the step most people skip, and it is the step that moves a word into long-term memory.

Do your twenty words at the same time every day — morning is best — and test yourself the same evening without looking. Words you get wrong go back into tomorrow's list; words you get right move to the weekly review pile. Then attempt twenty questions in English MCQs, so new words meet exam-style options, including near-miss distractors. Pair this with our 30-day English grammar plan and vocabulary becomes part of your daily English hour.

Use Words or Lose Them: Reading, Speaking and Writing

A word you only read is a word you will forget. A word you use is a word you own. Use new words three ways daily: read one editorial paragraph and spot your words in real text; say two or three new words aloud in sentences; and write one short paragraph forcing in three new words. It feels awkward for a week, then natural — exactly what exam recall feels like. Even fifteen minutes of daily editorial reading grows your passive vocabulary fast.

Spaced Revision: The Technique Behind Every Topper's Memory

Here is the uncomfortable truth about memory: you will forget most new words within a day unless you revise them on a schedule. The fix is spaced revision — reviewing after one day, then three days, then a week, then a month. Each review takes seconds, but each one doubles how long the word stays. Keep a small section in your error notebook for words you got wrong, and revise only that section on your weekly review day.

A simple weekly rhythm works well. Learn twenty new words Monday to Friday. On Saturday, revise all one hundred — cover the meaning, recall it, check. On Sunday, revise only the ones you missed. After three months you will hold over a thousand words.

Synonyms and Antonyms: How Examiners Set Traps

Most vocabulary marks are lost not on unknown words, but on traps. Examiners place a near-synonym beside the correct answer: for "candid", the options may include "honest" (correct) and "frank" (close, but the question asks for the nearest meaning). Read the stem carefully — "nearest in meaning" and "opposite in meaning" are different tasks. Note the key word before looking at the options, and note second meanings: "obscure" can mean unclear or unknown, and "novel" can mean new.

When you truly do not know a word, use elimination: remove the options you know are wrong, use roots to guess, and only then decide. With −0.25 marking, a 50-50 guess after elimination is worth it; a blind guess among four is not. Practise this in the exam simulator under time pressure.

5 Vocabulary Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Memorising lists without sentences. A word without a sentence has no hook. Always write your own example.
  2. Learning too many words at once. Fifty words a day feels productive and evaporates by night. Twenty, done properly, beats fifty skimmed.
  3. Skipping revision. Learning without spaced revision is pouring water into a leaking bucket. Saturday review is non-negotiable.
  4. Ignoring antonyms. Every synonym question has an antonym twin. Learn both directions, or half your preparation is missing.
  5. Studying vocabulary only before the exam. Vocabulary is a slow crop. Start now, even if the exam is months away.

Vocabulary rewards the steady learner, not the last-minute crammer. Twenty words a day, word families, roots, your own sentences, and a Saturday review — that is the whole secret. Start today with our 60-day study plan and free practice MCQs.

Key takeaways
  • Learn word families and roots, not isolated lists — one root unlocks many words.
  • Twenty words a day, each with your own sentence, one synonym and one antonym.
  • Revise on a spaced schedule: after 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, then 1 month.
  • Use new words daily — read, speak and write them.
  • Learn antonyms with synonyms, watch for trap options, eliminate before guessing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many English words should I learn for PPSC?

Aim for 1,000 to 1,500 well-known exam words rather than 5,000 half-known ones. Twenty words a day for three months gets you there.

How do I remember words I keep forgetting?

Put them in your error notebook, give them a personal example sentence, and revise them on the spaced schedule — day 1, day 3, week 1, month 1.

Can I improve vocabulary without a coaching academy?

Yes. Daily reading, the 20-word system and free MCQ practice are enough — consistency matters more than coaching.
