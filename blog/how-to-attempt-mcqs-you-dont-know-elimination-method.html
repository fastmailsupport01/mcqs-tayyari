How to Attempt MCQs You Don't Know: The Elimination Method

📅 November 7, 2026✍️ MCQs Tayyari Team⏱️ 8 min read

Every aspirant meets them in the exam hall: questions that look completely unfamiliar. You read the stem twice, stare at four options, and realise you simply do not know the answer. In a 100-MCQ paper, this happens to everyone — even toppers face 15 to 25 such questions. The difference between selection and rejection is what toppers do with what they don't know. That is where the elimination method comes in: instead of searching your memory for the right answer, you hunt for the wrong ones and cross them out, one by one. A question you knew nothing about becomes a 50-50 choice — and in a paper with negative marking, that shift changes the entire maths of your score.

What Is the Elimination Method?

The elimination method is simple: when you cannot identify the correct option directly, you remove the options you are confident are wrong. Every option you eliminate improves your odds. With four options, a blind guess has a 25% chance. Eliminate one wrong option and you are at 33%. Eliminate two and you are at 50%. Eliminate three and you have found the answer without ever "knowing" it.

This is not luck. Examiners write distractors that are wrong for a reason: a date a decade off, a fact from the wrong country, a term from the wrong subject. Your job is to spot those fingerprints, not to recall the textbook line. It works best in General Ability — General Knowledge, Everyday Science, Pakistan Studies, Islamiat — where your half-knowledge is real: you may not remember the exact answer, but you often remember enough to know what it is not.

The 4-Step Elimination Method

Use the same four steps on every unknown question.

Step 1: Read the stem twice — and read all four options

Half of all elimination failures start with a misread question. Watch for "NOT", "first", "largest" or "except", and read every option before deciding.

Step 2: Cross out the obviously wrong options

Cross out options on your question paper, starting with the clearly impossible one: the wrong era, country or unit. If you are 80% sure an option is wrong, strike it.

Step 3: Compare what is left

Look only at the survivors. Often two options are near-identical — the answer is one of them, and the difference between them is the detail to test. Our General Knowledge MCQs are full of these paired-option traps.

Step 4: Decide by the 50-50 rule

If two options remain, the decision depends on negative marking. In the PPSC one paper test, a wrong answer costs 0.25 marks, so a 50-50 guess has an expected value of +0.375 marks — attempt it. If three or four options remain, the expected value turns negative — skip it and come back in the second pass. Our negative marking strategy guide explains the maths in full.

4 Elimination Clues Examiners Leave Behind

Distractors are written quickly, and quick writing leaves patterns:

  • Absolute words: options with "always", "never", "all" or "none" are wrong far more often than right.
  • The odd one out: if three options belong to one category (three rivers, three capitals) and one does not, the odd one is usually the distractor — or the answer.
  • Grammatical mismatch: if the stem asks "Who was the first..." and one option is a place, not a person, strike it.
  • Overly specific numbers: a suspiciously precise figure ("1,847 km" against three round numbers) is often invented.

None of these clues is a guarantee — but two nudges pointing the same way are enough to eliminate an option with confidence.

Elimination in Different Subjects

The method bends to fit the subject. In Everyday Science, eliminate by unit and scale: if the question asks for a vitamin and one option is a mineral, it is gone — practise this in our Everyday Science MCQs. In Pakistan Studies, eliminate by era: a 1947 event cannot involve a 1962 constitution. In English, eliminate by grammar rule first — two of the four options usually break the same rule. In Basic Mathematics, eliminate by estimation: round the numbers and strike any option that is wildly off — our Basic Mathematics practice section builds this habit. The same method applies across every exam — see our PPSC preparation resources and FPSC preparation resources for test-specific patterns.

A Worked Example

Imagine this question: "Which is the largest desert in the world?" Options: (A) Sahara (B) Gobi (C) Arabian (D) Kalahari. You are not sure — but you know the Gobi is smaller than the Sahara, the Arabian is a regional desert, and the Kalahari is far smaller. Cross out B, C and D on size alone, and Sahara is left standing. You never "recalled" the answer — you eliminated your way to it.

Practice Drills That Make Elimination Automatic

Elimination only helps if it is fast. Train it like any other skill:

  1. The strike-through drill: solve 50 mixed MCQs, crossing out at least one option on every question before choosing — even the ones you know.
  2. The two-option drill: in a mock test, note how many options you eliminated on every unsure question, then check whether your accuracy rose with more eliminations.
  3. The timed second pass: in every PPSC mock test, reserve the last 20 minutes for skipped questions — see the timing plan in our 90-minute time management guide.

Log your elimination decisions in your error notebook — especially the questions where you eliminated the right answer.

4 Mistakes That Break the Elimination Method

  1. Eliminating on a hunch, not a reason. "This feels wrong" is not elimination. Every strike needs a reason — a wrong date, a wrong unit, a broken rule.
  2. Eliminating the correct answer early. Striking an option because it looks unfamiliar is the most common error. Unfamiliar is not wrong — the right answer is often the option you have never seen.
  3. Guessing with three options left. With three options left, a random guess loses marks on average under −0.25. Skip it.
  4. Changing answers without new evidence. First instincts after elimination beat changed answers. Change one only for a concrete reason — a fact you remembered, a clue you missed.
Key takeaways
  • Elimination turns unknown questions into 50-50 choices — cross out wrong options instead of hunting for the right one.
  • Two options left: attempt it. Three or more left: skip it.
  • Every elimination needs a reason — a wrong date, unit or rule — never a hunch.
  • Train it with strike-through drills and timed second passes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I attempt MCQs I don't know in the PPSC exam?

Yes — but only after eliminating options. At two options left, the maths favours attempting it despite the −0.25 negative marking. Blind guessing loses marks, so skip those.

What is the elimination method in MCQ exams?

Instead of recalling the right answer, you cross out the wrong options one by one — each elimination raises your guessing chance, from 25% to 50%.

How do I know which MCQ option to eliminate first?

Start with the option that contradicts a fact you are sure about — a wrong date, country or unit.

Does the elimination method work for FPSC and NTS tests too?

Yes — any four-option MCQ test. In NTS tests, which usually have no negative marking, attempt every question after eliminating what you can.
