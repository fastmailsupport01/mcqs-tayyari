NTS Test Preparation Guide 2026: Pattern & Tips

📅 October 20, 2026✍️ MCQs Tayyari Team⏱️ 9 min read

Every year, the National Testing Service quietly decides the fate of hundreds of thousands of candidates — university admissions, HEC scholarships, and countless screening tests for jobs all run through NTS. Yet most aspirants prepare for it exactly like a board exam: reading books, memorizing facts, and hoping. That approach fails, because the NTS test is not a knowledge exam — it's an aptitude exam. It tests how fast and clearly you think under a clock, not how much you have memorized.

The good news: aptitude is trainable. A clear pattern map plus a few weeks of deliberate practice can genuinely add 15–20 marks to your score. This guide covers the 2026 NTS test pattern, a section-wise strategy, a realistic 4-week plan, and the no-negative-marking playbook that separates toppers from the crowd.

Which NTS Test Are You Appearing In?

NTS conducts several different tests, and knowing which one you're sitting for shapes your preparation. The big three:

  • NAT (National Aptitude Test) — for undergraduate university admissions. NAT-I carries 90 MCQs; NAT-II carries 100 MCQs.
  • GAT (Graduate Assessment Test) General — for MPhil/PhD admissions and HEC scholarships. The standard pattern: 100 MCQs in 120 minutes.
  • Screening / recruitment tests — organizations and departments outsource their hiring tests to NTS. The pattern varies, but the core sections are strikingly similar to NAT/GAT.

Despite the different names, all of these share the same DNA: Verbal Reasoning (English), Quantitative Reasoning (math), and Analytical Reasoning (logic puzzles) — sometimes plus a subject-specific portion. Our NTS preparation section follows exactly this structure.

The NTS Test Pattern You Must Memorize

For GAT-General and NAT-II, the pattern is consistent and worth committing to memory:

  • Total questions: 100 MCQs
  • Total marks: 100 (1 mark per question)
  • Time allowed: 120 minutes
  • Negative marking: none — wrong answers cost you nothing
  • Qualifying threshold: generally around 50%

Two numbers matter most. First: 72 seconds per question — more generous than PPSC's 54 seconds, but the questions are trickier and puzzles eat time silently. Second: zero negative marking — the fact the entire playbook below is built on.

Section-Wise Strategy: How Each Part Actually Works

Verbal Reasoning — the highest-return section

English carries heavy weight in every NTS test: synonyms and antonyms, analogies, sentence completion, reading comprehension, and grammar correction. This section rewards vocabulary built over weeks, not nights. Learn 20 new words daily with example sentences, and practice analogies as relationship-spotting (king:queen :: actor:actress). Our English MCQ practice covers exactly these formats.

Quantitative Reasoning — speed math, not deep math

NTS quantitative is percentages, ratios, averages, simple algebra, and basic geometry — the same formulas, disguised in story problems. The skill is translation: converting a wordy scenario into an equation in seconds. Drill short sets of 15 questions against a 15-minute timer daily, and memorize the classic shortcuts (percentage-to-fraction conversions, average tricks). Sharpen this with our Basic Mathematics practice.

Analytical Reasoning — the learnable "impossible" section

Seven people sitting in a row with conditions... grouping puzzles... sequencing games. This section terrifies first-timers, but it's the most coachable part of the paper: every puzzle type repeats with different names. Learn the 5–6 standard puzzle families, practice drawing quick diagrams instead of solving in your head, and time yourself ruthlessly. Improvement here is often the fastest of all sections.

Subject portion — where applicable

Some NTS tests include a subject-specific part (your degree subject). Don't let it steal time from the three reasoning sections — a light revision of core concepts plus MCQ practice is enough. Browse all subjects to find your field.

Your 4-Week Study Plan

Four genuinely focused weeks are enough to transform an NTS score. Here's the skeleton — scale the hours to your routine:

  1. Week 1: Diagnose & build English. Take one full mock cold to find your baseline. Start the 20-words-a-day habit. Revise quantitative formulas and do one timed analytical puzzle set daily.
  2. Week 2: Quantitative speed. Daily timed sets of 15 quantitative questions. Continue vocabulary. Learn each analytical puzzle family one by one — quality over quantity.
  3. Week 3: Mixed volume. 100+ mixed MCQs daily across all three sections. Full reading-comprehension practice under time. Start reviewing every wrong answer the same day — the error notebook is gold.
  4. Week 4: Simulation. Three full 100-MCQ mocks in exactly 120 minutes, mirroring real conditions. Analyze each mock for time leaks, not just wrong answers. Revise only weak areas in the final 3 days — no new topics.

The No-Negative-Marking Playbook

NTS's biggest gift is that wrong answers cost nothing — yet most candidates leave marks on the table. The playbook:

  • Attempt everything. With no penalty, a blank answer and a wrong answer are identical: zero. An educated guess is strictly better than a skip.
  • Eliminate, then guess. Cross out the obviously wrong options first. Even removing one option raises a guess from 25% to 33% — across 20 guessed questions, that's real marks.
  • Two-pass method. First pass (70 min): answer everything you can solve confidently; mark the rest. Second pass (30 min): return with fresh eyes to puzzles and hard questions. Final 20 min: review marks and fill every remaining blank.
  • Never surrender to a puzzle. If a logic puzzle eats 3 minutes, mark it, move on, and come back. Easy English marks later in the paper are worth exactly the same as the puzzle.
  • Practice exactly like this. If you practice mocks with negative marking turned on, you're training for the wrong game. Use our NTS exam simulator with the real pattern: 100 MCQs, 120 minutes, no penalty.

5 Mistakes That Sink NTS Scores

  1. Preparing like it's a board exam. Reading theory chapters instead of solving timed MCQs is the #1 reason smart students score poorly on aptitude tests.
  2. Leaving vocabulary for the last week. Verbal reasoning is the heaviest section and the slowest to build. Start it on day one.
  3. Solving puzzles in your head. Always draw the quick diagram. Head-solving is slower and far more error-prone under pressure.
  4. No full-length timed practice. Section-wise practice builds skills; only full 120-minute mocks build stamina and pacing.
  5. Leaving questions blank. With no negative marking, an unattempted question is a donated mark. There is no excuse for a blank answer sheet.
Key takeaways
  • NAT/GAT pattern: 100 MCQs · 120 minutes · no negative marking · ~50% qualifying.
  • Three core sections — Verbal (English), Quantitative, Analytical — plus a subject part on some tests.
  • No penalty means: attempt everything, eliminate then guess, never leave blanks.
  • Train aptitude, not memory — timed MCQ practice beats theory reading every time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there negative marking in NTS tests?

No — NTS MCQ tests generally carry no negative marking, which is a major strategic difference from PPSC and FPSC papers. Always double-check the instructions printed on your specific test's admission slip, but the standard pattern has no penalty for wrong answers.

What is the difference between NAT and GAT?

NAT (National Aptitude Test) is for undergraduate university admissions, while GAT (Graduate Assessment Test) General is for MPhil/PhD admissions and scholarships such as HEC's. Both test verbal, quantitative, and analytical reasoning in MCQ format.

How many marks are needed to pass the NTS GAT?

The qualifying threshold is generally around 50%, but for admissions and scholarships what matters is your rank and merit position, not just passing — aim well above the cutoff.
