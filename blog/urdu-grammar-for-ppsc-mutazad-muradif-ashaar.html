Urdu Grammar for PPSC: Mutazad, Muradif & Famous Ashaar

📅 October 26, 2026✍️ MCQs Tayyari Team⏱️ 8 min read

Urdu is the section most PPSC aspirants take for granted — and that is exactly why it is the easiest place to pick up marks. Everybody speaks Urdu, so everybody assumes they know it. But the one-paper test does not ask whether you can speak Urdu. It asks whether you know the mutazad (opposite) of a word, the muradif (synonym) of another, and which poet wrote a famous sher. These are memory questions — the cheapest marks on the paper.

This guide covers the four Urdu topics that appear again and again in PPSC papers — mutazad alfaz, muradif alfaz, famous ashaar with their poets, and mahavray — plus the small traps (tazkeer-o-tanees, wahid-jama) that separate toppers from the rest.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Urdu grammar is a memory section — revise word lists daily, don't just read them once.
  • Mutazad and muradif carry the most Urdu questions — learn them as pairs.
  • For ashaar, memorise the poet with the sher — PPSC asks "who wrote this?"
  • Attempt Urdu questions in timed Urdu MCQ practice — speed matters as much as knowledge.

What PPSC Actually Asks in Urdu

The Urdu portion of the General Ability part is short — usually a handful of questions — but almost entirely predictable:

  • Mutazad alfaz: "What is the opposite of roshan?" (Answer: tareek.)
  • Muradif alfaz: "Which word means the same as dost?" (Answer: rafeeq or saathi.)
  • Ashaar and poets: "This sher is by which poet?" — the sher is given, you name the poet.
  • Mahavray (idioms): "What does aankh ka tara mean?" (Someone extremely dear.)
  • Tazkeer-o-tanees and wahid-jama: is a word masculine or feminine, singular or plural?

Mutazad Alfaz: Learn Them as Pairs

The biggest mistake with mutazad alfaz is memorising one side of the pair. If you learn that roshan means "bright" but never link it to tareek, you will hesitate when the question asks for the opposite. Learn antonyms as a locked pair, and say both words out loud together:

Two practical tips. First, PPSC loves tricky pairs where the opposite is not the first word that comes to mind — the mutazad of aam (common) is khaas (special). Second, when unsure, eliminate options that are merely different words rather than true opposites. Drill these pairs in our Urdu MCQ practice section until answers come automatically.

Muradif Alfaz: Near Meaning Is Not Enough

Muradif questions look easy and are the easiest to get wrong. The trap is the near synonym: an option that feels related but is not the accepted muradif. The muradif of mohabbat is ulfat, not dosti — related, but not the same meaning. Train on the standard pairs:

  • Dost — rafeeq, saathi
  • Khushi — musarrat, shadmani
  • Gham — ranj, dukh
  • Roshni — noor, ujala
  • Chand — mahtaab, qamar
  • Safar — musafrat, raah

A simple memory trick: group muradif by theme — light words together (roshni, noor, ujala), sky words together (aasman, falak, chand, mahtaab) — instead of one long alphabetical list. Grouped words are recalled far better under pressure. And if two options both look like synonyms, pick the one matching the given word's register — a poetic word like mahtaab pairs with chand, not with an everyday word.

Famous Ashaar: Memorise the Poet, Not Just the Sher

Here is how PPSC tests ashaar: it prints a famous sher and asks which poet wrote it. Candidates who only memorised the sher's meaning fail; candidates who linked every sher to its poet pass. So attach the poet's name like a label to every sher you learn. Start with the four poets who appear most often:

  • Mir Taqi Mir — "Dikhai diye yun ke bekhud kiya, hamen aap se bhi juda kar chale."
  • Mirza Ghalib — "Dil-e-nadan tujhe hua kya hai, aakhir is dard ki dawa kya hai."
  • Allama Iqbal — "Khudi ko kar buland itna ke har taqdeer se pehle, Khuda bande se khud pooche bata teri raza kya hai."
  • Faiz Ahmed Faiz — "Hum ke thehrey ajnabi itni madaaraton ke baad, phir banenge aashna kitni mulaqaton ke baad."

Do not try to memorise whole divans — the most common time-waster in Urdu preparation. Twenty famous ashaar, each firmly linked to its poet, cover most of what the paper asks. Add one new sher daily, and every Sunday test yourself both ways: poet to sher, and sher to poet. Our free Urdu eBook carries a 100-MCQ Urdu set for this drill.

Mahavray, Tazkeer-o-Tanees & the Small Traps

Mahavray (idioms) are short, high-yield questions — one idiom, one meaning, one mark. Learn the meaning as a picture, not a definition: aankh ka tara is the person you look at with love; aasteen ka saanp is the enemy hiding close to you; darya ko koozay mein band karna is fitting a big idea into few words; baal baal bachna is a narrow escape. Pictures stick; definitions fade.

Then come two quiet traps. Tazkeer-o-tanees (gender): kitaab is feminine and qalam is masculine — the paper tests exactly these everyday words. Wahid-jama (singular-plural): kitaab becomes kutub, lafz becomes alfaaz, sher becomes ashaar. Learn the irregular plurals.

Your Two-Week Urdu Grammar Plan

  1. Days 1–3: Mutazad + muradif foundation. Learn 10 pairs of each per day, by hand — writing beats reading for word lists.
  2. Days 4–6: Ashaar + mahavray. Add 5 ashaar (with poets) and 5 mahavray per day. Test yourself every evening.
  3. Days 7–10: Traps + volume. Cover tazkeer-o-tanees and wahid-jama lists, then attempt 50 Urdu MCQs daily from our Urdu practice section.
  4. Days 11–14: Simulation. Mix Urdu questions into full 100-MCQ practice sets under time pressure, revising only your error notebook in the last two days.

Keep one rule throughout: every word you get wrong goes into your error notebook the same day. Urdu preparation fails not because the syllabus is big — it is small — but because candidates revise what they already know and skip what they keep getting wrong. Frequently Asked Questions

How many Urdu questions come in the PPSC one-paper test?

Urdu sits in the General Ability portion, so the count varies by post — but a handful of questions on mutazad, muradif, ashaar and mahavray appear in almost every paper.

What is the difference between mutazad and muradif alfaz?

Mutazad alfaz are opposites — roshan/tareek, sach/jhoot. Muradif alfaz are synonyms — dost/rafeeq, khushi/musarrat. Learn both as pairs.

Which poets' ashaar should I prepare for PPSC?

Start with Mir Taqi Mir, Ghalib, Iqbal and Faiz — their famous ashaar are asked most often. Twenty ashaar, each linked to its poet, cover most of what the paper asks.
